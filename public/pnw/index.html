Worth a lawyer

Your dealership is still the seller of record.

Every listing is titled “By PNW Used Mobile Homes” and described as “posted by PNW Used Mobile Homes.” If the point of the pivot is not being the dealer of record, the site does not yet read that way.

Compounding

The SEO foundations are off.

No sitemap. One boilerplate description shared by every listing. Photo alt text reads “Photo #1” to “Photo #10.” Listing pages are typed as a generic collection, not a home for sale.

What we concluded: the platform stays

We would not rebuild. The bottleneck is not technical — two listings and no buyer traffic is a pricing and inventory problem, and no amount of custom code changes that. Brilliant Directories already does what the proving phase needs: free plans, self-serve listings, dealer accounts, a banner ad manager, sitemaps, schema. Nothing we found is blocked by it. Rebuilding now would be exactly the overbuilding you said you wanted to avoid.

Its real ceiling, so you know where it is before you meet it: no code export, the data model cannot be restructured, no arbitrary API integrations, a listing cap somewhere between 500 and 2,500 depending on plan, theme-level design changes only, and roughly $40–120 a month per site if you replicate to other regions. We revisit when one of those actually binds — most likely when automation or a second region becomes the priority.

What we concluded: the $1,000 milestone is close enough to touch

At your existing rates that is roughly two sponsored articles at $500, or about ten dealers at $99 a month, or a blend with banner inventory. Ten paying dealers is well within reach for someone with your relationships. The constraint is not finding dealers — it is having audience evidence to sell them. Which is why inventory comes first and outreach comes second.

Smaller things we also found

A stray fragment of template code renders inside the contact form on listing pages. The “Browse Homes” menu link has a trailing space inside the link itself. Pages weigh 214 KB to 383 KB before a single photo loads. The site advertises “3,000+ monthly visitors” and “join hundreds of sellers” — with two listings live, those are hard to defend if an advertiser asks, and there is a question about it below.

Where Jev fits.

No chatbot. No AI sprinkled across the site. Jev is a different kind of model — it reads a situation and returns a typed answer with a probability, rather than writing text. Code keeps the arithmetic, the rules and the permissions. That makes it fast enough and cheap enough to sit in the middle of ordinary workflows, including yours, not just the website’s.

Your industry suits this unusually well: the facts a buyer can see are not the facts that decide whether they can buy. Transport, park approval, lot rent, age rules, financing eligibility — messy, regional, often unstated. That is where this kind of judgment earns its place, and it is what a national site does not do.

  1. Dealer inventory, imported however the dealer keeps it

    This is the one a first month lives or dies on. A dealer already has inventory — in a spreadsheet, on their own website, in a PDF, in a folder of photos with a price scrawled in the filename. Today that means someone retypes it, which is why dealers do not bother. Instead: the dealer sends whatever they have, the model works out which values are the year, the make, the size, the price and the location, and code writes the listings and asks about only what is genuinely ambiguous.

    Why it matters: it turns your ask to a dealer from “please learn our system and type in forty homes” into “send me your list.” That difference is most of whether the site has inventory in thirty days.

  2. A listing a seller can finish in five minutes

    Writing the description is where a private seller gives up, and it is the thing they do worst. Seller uploads photos and a few facts; the model judges what each photo shows and whether the described condition matches them; code assembles the listing, the alt text and the photo order. The seller edits rather than composes.

  3. A quality gate, because free listings attract junk

    The moment listings are free this stops being optional. Is this a real home, a duplicate of one already on the site, a dealer wearing a private-seller hat, or a familiar scam shape? Code owns the decision: publish, hold for a human, or refuse. You see a short queue, not an inbox.

  4. Answering the question buyers actually have

    A buyer is not really asking “three beds under $80,000.” They are asking whether they can put this home where they need it, and what it truly costs once it is there. The model reads their situation in their own words; code does the arithmetic and the hard rules — a pre-1976 home’s financing status is a rule, not a judgment.

    This is the most defensible thing a regional site can be better at, precisely because the constraints are regional.

  5. Turning an enquiry into something an advertiser will pay for

    Most enquiries carry a second need: moving the home, financing it, a space to put it in, a setup crew. Routing a qualified referral to the transporter, lender or installer paying for that category is worth considerably more than a banner impression — and it monetizes enquiries rather than page views, which matters while page views are the thing you do not yet have.

  6. Keeping the inventory honest

    Free listings rot. Homes sell and nobody takes the listing down. Flag what looks sold, stale or priced far from comparable homes, and nudge the seller. Stale inventory is what makes buyers stop trusting a marketplace, usually before anyone notices.

One honest limit: Brilliant Directories does not open its data model or let us integrate arbitrary APIs. The first three of these can run alongside it — work happens outside, finished listings go in. The last three want more room, and they are a good part of the answer to “when would we outgrow this platform.” Not on a feeling: when four, five and six are worth building.
