What I concluded: build on what is there now, but do not marry it

The bottleneck today is not technical. Two listings and no buyer traffic is a pricing and inventory problem, and no amount of custom code fixes that. So month one happens on Brilliant Directories: paywall down, funnel fixed, SEO foundations repaired, inventory seeded. None of that is wasted whatever we decide later, because the value is listings and traffic, and both of those move.

But I am not pretending the platform is fine. It is the most constrained option available. The data model cannot be changed, so the things that actually matter in your industry — must be moved, park approval, age rules, transport distance, lot rent — can never be proper fields. There are no custom integrations, so three of the four ideas further down — answering the buyer’s real question, routing enquiries to businesses that will pay for them, and keeping listings honest — cannot be built inside it. There is no code export, so the site is not an asset you could ever sell on its own.

So I hedge, and the hedge is nearly free. I build the dealer import outside Brilliant Directories from the first day. It writes finished listings into your site, but the code and the listing data are yours — not locked inside Brilliant Directories — which means if we move, the pipeline and everything it has collected move with it.

I want to be precise about why, because the usual version of this argument is not the one I am making. It is not that rebuilding is slow or expensive — see the next section. It is that a platform built away from real demand is guesswork, however good the guesser. Build it while dealers are actually sending you homes and the details come to you instead of being invented. That is the whole reason to start on what exists.

What I should say plainly: building is not the bottleneck it used to be

Making it effortless to list.

This is where I think the real strategy is, and it comes straight from your own advantage. You have the relationships. So the product's whole job is to turn a relationship into inventory with no more friction than sending a text.

Every marketplace dies of a supply problem, and the supply problem is almost always the form. A dealer with forty homes will not fill in forty forms. A private seller looking at a photo uploader closes the tab.

So: no form. A dealer sends whatever they already have, in whatever shape they already have it, and a listing appears.

  • Text or WhatsApp the photos inDealers already send inventory this way. No new habit to learn.
  • Forward an emailWhatever they already emailed someone else about the home.
  • Paste a linkTheir listing on another site, or a page on their own website.
  • Drop a spreadsheet, a PDF or a folder of photosForty homes in one go, in whatever shape they keep them.
  • Snap photos on a phoneFor the seller standing in the home right now. No account needed to start.

Behind all of those, the model reads the inputs and works out which value is the year, the make, the size, the price, the location — what each photo shows, whether the condition described matches them, whether two submissions are the same home. Then a listing page appears for them to correct rather than compose. Nothing is published until they say so.

The one that changes the conversation

Give the site one link and it keeps itself in sync. A dealer points it at their own inventory page, or their profile elsewhere. A new home appears there, it appears here. Sold there, it comes down here. Their ongoing effort is zero, because they already maintain that page for other reasons.

That turns your ask to a dealer from “please do some work for me” into “we will do the work for you, for free, permanently.” For someone deciding whether to bother with a new regional site, that is a completely different question. One caution: mirroring a dealer’s listings from someone else’s platform needs their written permission and still touches that platform’s rules — their own website or a file they send over is cleaner, and I would start there.

Why it should feel good, not just be quick

They see a listing, not a form. It looks better than what they would have made themselves — photos straightened and ordered, a hero image chosen, the description in clean English. It is fast enough to feel like a trick. And they get a link they actually want to send to buyers, which is how the site spreads without anyone marketing it.

If their listing on your site is the best-looking version of their home anywhere, and it took ninety seconds, the supply problem solves itself. That is the bet.

What would make it better than MHVillage.

Being regional is a start, but it is not a reason for anyone to switch. The listing experience above is the first and most important — none of the rest matters on a site with no homes on it. These four are what would make people choose it once the homes are there — and the reason all four are possible here is that the constraints in your industry are local, which is exactly what a national site cannot handle.

For buyers

Answer the question buyers are really asking.

Nobody is really searching for “three beds under $80,000.” They are asking whether they can put this home where they need it, and what it actually costs once it is there — transport, park approval, lot rent, age rules, whether anyone will finance it at all.

A buyer describes their situation in their own words, and each home comes back as feasible, feasible if something changes, or blocked and why. Not a list of near-misses they have to phone around about.

The same thing is the search box. Somebody types what actually matters to them — “something I can move onto my land in Kitsap County, under $90k, that a bank will lend on” — and the homes that fit come back in about a second. No filters to translate their life into, and no filter menu that has to anticipate every situation in advance.

This is the one MHVillage cannot copy, because the rules are different in every county.

For revenue · worth a lawyer

Turn every enquiry into something a business will pay for.

Almost every buyer enquiry carries a second need behind it: moving the home, somewhere to put it, a crew to set it up, insurance.

Spot which, and send that person — with their permission — to the business that wants them. A transporter will pay far more for ten people who genuinely need a home moved than for a month of banner space. It also earns money from enquiries rather than page views, which matters while page views are the thing you do not yet have.

One caution, and it is a real one. Paid referral of a buyer to a lender is regulated in a way that referring them to a transporter is not — referral-fee rules and state licensing both bite, and manufactured-home lending has its own wrinkles. Getting their permission is not the answer to it. So: transporters, installers, parks and insurers first, and lenders only after your attorney has told me how.

For trust

Every listing checked before it appears.

The day listings become free, the junk arrives: duplicates, dealers posing as private sellers, and the scam patterns this industry knows too well. Unmoderated junk is what kills a young marketplace’s reputation, usually before anyone notices.

So every submission gets checked — publish, hold for you, or refuse. You see a short queue, not an inbox.

And “every listing on this site is checked” becomes something you can say out loud in a market where buyers are genuinely afraid of being cheated. That is worth more here than more inventory.

For quality

Inventory that stays honest by itself.

Free listings rot. Homes sell and nobody comes back to take the listing down, and a buyer who calls about three sold homes in a row does not call a fourth.

Listings that look sold, stale, or priced far from comparable homes get flagged and the seller gets a nudge. Nothing disappears silently.

How these actually work, if you want the detail

Not a chatbot, and not AI sprinkled over the site. The model I would use is called Jev, and it works differently from the ones you have seen write text: it reads a situation and returns a specific typed answer with a confidence attached, rather than producing paragraphs. It is fast and cheap enough to sit inside ordinary steps rather than being a feature you visit.

Everything with a right answer stays in ordinary code — the arithmetic, the distances, the permissions, and every hard rule. Whether a pre-1976 home can be financed is a rule, not an opinion, and it is written as one. The model is only asked the questions that need judgment: which value in this spreadsheet is the year, what does this photo show, is this the same home as one already on the site, does this listing read as genuine.

Where something has to be written rather than decided — a description from a set of facts — a small cheap model does that, and only that. It never decides a fact; it phrases facts that have already been established. That boundary is what stops it inventing a bedroom.

The practical consequence: the confidence number decides what happens next. High confidence publishes, middling asks you one question, low holds it for review. So the system gets more autonomous as it earns it, rather than being trusted on day one.

One honest limit: your current platform cannot do three of these four — answering the buyer’s real question, routing enquiries, and keeping listings honest. It does not open its data model or allow the integrations they need. Checking every listing can run alongside it, and so can the listing engine above. The rest is what the platform decision is really about.
